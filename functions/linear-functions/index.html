3.2 Linear Functions

Linear functions are among the simplest and most important functions encountered in mathematics, engineering and science. Many physical systems may be approximated by linear relationships over a limited range. Examples include:

  • Distance travelled at constant speed.

  • Cost as a function of quantity purchased.

  • Electrical relationships such as Ohm’s Law.

  • Conversion between temperature scales.

The graph of a linear function is always a straight line, for example

3.2.1 The General Form of a Linear Function

Definition

A linear function may be written in the form

\[ y=mx+c \]

where \(m\) is the gradient and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.

The variable \(x\) is the independent variable and \(y\) is the dependent variable.

3.2.2 The Gradient

The gradient describes the steepness of a line.

Definition

The gradient of a straight line is

\[ m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} =\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} \]

where \(\Delta y\) is the vertical change and \(\Delta x\) is the horizontal change.

The gradient tells us how much \(y\) changes when \(x\) increases by one unit.

Worked Example

Determine the gradient of the line passing through the points \((0,2)\) and \((6,14)\).

Here \((x_1,y_1)=(0,2)\) and \((x_2,y_2)=(6,14)\).

3.2.3 Positive and Negative Gradients

A line with positive gradient \((m\gt 0)\) rises from left to right. A line with negative gradient (\(m\lt 0\)) falls from left to right. If the graph is horizontal, the gradient is zero (\(m=0\)).

Worked Example

Determine the gradient of the line passing through \((2,10)\) and \((10,-6)\).

Here \((x_1,y_1)=(2,10)\) and \((x_2,y_2)=(10,-6)\).

Therefore the gradient is \(m=-5\). The line decreases from left to right.

3.2.4 The \(y\)-Intercept

The \(y\)-intercept is the value of \(y\) when \(x=0\).

Definition

For the linear function

\[ y=mx+c \]

the constant \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.

The graph crosses the \(y\)-axis at

\[ (0,c) \]
Worked Example

Determine the \(y\)-intercept of \(y=3x+4\).

Comparing with \(y=mx+c\) gives \(c=4\). Therefore the line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0,4)\).

3.2.5 The \(x\)-Intercept

The \(x\)-intercept occurs where the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis. At this point \(y=0\).

Worked Example

Find the \(x\)-intercept of \(y=2x+8\).

Let \(y=0\).

Therefore the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((-4,0)\).

3.2.7 Finding the Equation of a Straight Line

Sometimes the graph of a line is known and we wish to determine its equation. The procedure is:

  1. Determine the gradient.

  2. Determine the \(y\)-intercept.

  3. Substitute into the general form of a linear line: \(y=mx+c\).

Worked Example

A line passes through \((1,3)\) and \((5,11)\). Find its equation.

First determine the gradient.

Substitute into \(y=mx+c\) to give

\[ y=2x+c \]

Use the point \((1,3)\) to find \(c\).

Therefore \(y=2x+1\).

3.2.8 Parallel Lines

Parallel lines have the same gradient.

Definition
Two straight lines are parallel if they have equal gradients.

For example,

\[ y=2x+3 \]

and

\[ y=2x-5 \]

are parallel because both have gradient \(m=2\).

3.2.9 Engineering Application

Engineering Application

For constant speed motion,

\[ d=vt \]

where \(d\) is distance, \(v\) is speed and \(t\) is time. Suppose a train travels at \(30\, \mathrm{m/s}\).Then

\[ d=30t \]

3.2.10 Common Mistakes

Common errors include:

  • Confusing the gradient and intercept.

  • Forgetting that the gradient is \( \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \) and not \( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta y} \).

  • Using only one coordinate when calculating a gradient.

  • Forgetting to set \(y=0\) when finding an \(x\)-intercept.

  • Assuming all straight lines pass through the origin.

Introduction

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  • Recognise polynomial, exponential and logarithmic functions.

  • Recognise the typical graphs of these functions.

  • Determine the equation of a straight line from a graph or coordinates.

  • Apply the quadratic formula.

  • Use logarithms to determine unknown powers.

  • Solve exponential equations.

Polynomials

Recognising Polynomials

  • A polynomial function is one that only involves non-negative integer powers of \(x\), for example:

    • \(y=7x+4\) (polynomial of order/degree 1, linear)

    • \(y=3x^2-5x-1\) (polynomial of order/degree 2, quadratic)

    • \(y=-x^3+5x^2-7x+12.01\) (poly. of order/degree 3, cubic)

  • Functions containing negative or non-integer powers, or other functions, (such as trigonometric) are not polynomials, e.g.

    • \(y=x^2+4\sqrt{x}-5\) \(y=\dfrac {5}{x^2}-7x^3+6x-4\)

    • \(y=x^{2} + \sin (x)\)

Graphs of Polynomials: Constant functions

Graphs of constant functions (no dependency on \(x\)) are always straight, horizontal lines.

Graphs of Polynomials: Linear functions

Graphs of linear functions are always straight lines.

Graphs of Polynomials: Quadratic functions

Quadratic curves are either \(\cup \) or \(\cap \)-shaped depending on the values of \(a\).

Linear Functions

Determining the Linear Equation

Equation of a straight line

\begin{equation*} y=mx+c \end{equation*}

represents a straight line, where \(m\) and \(c\) are constants.

\(m\) is the gradient (slope) of the line and can be calculated as

\begin{equation*} m=\dfrac {\text{vertical change}}{\text{horizontal change}} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}= \dfrac {\Delta y}{\Delta x} \end{equation*}

\(c\) is the value of \(y\) when the line crosses the \(y\)-axis (at \(x=0\)), known as the \(y\)-intercept.

Note: to find where the line crosses the \(x\)-axis, simply let \(y=0\).

Quadratic Functions

Quadratic Functions

All quadratics can be represented in the general form:

The quadratic equation

\begin{equation*} y=ax^2+bx+c, \end{equation*}

where \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are constants and \(a \neq 0\).

Quadratic Functions

The constant \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept, as in the linear case (if \(x=0\) then the equation becomes \(y=a \times 0^2 + b \times 0 +c = c\)).

The curve can cross the \(x\)-axis (at \(y=0\)) twice, once (just touching it) or never. If it does cross the \(x\)-axis, we can calculate the values of \(x\) where this occurs by solving:

\begin{equation*} ax^2+bx+c = 0 \end{equation*}

The solutions of \(0=ax^2+bx+c\) are the same as the \(x\)-intercepts of \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) and are also known as the roots of \(ax^2+bx+c\).

The Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula: If \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) and \(a \neq 0\) then:

The discriminant is the “bit under the square root.” It indicates how many roots exist and of what type:

  • \(b^2-4ac \gt 0\) indicates two real and distinct roots (\(x_1\) and \(x_2\))

  • \(b^2-4ac =0\) indicates real and repeated roots (\(x_{1} = x_{2}\))

  • \(b^2-4ac \lt 0\) indicates complex roots (\(x=p\pm j q\))

Roots and Discriminants

Furthermore, if there are two distinct, real roots \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) to the quadratic \(y=ax^2+bx+c\), then it is possible to re-write the quadratic in the form:

\begin{equation*} y=a(x-x_1)(x-x_2) \end{equation*}

However, if there are real and repeated roots, \(x_1=x_2\), then it is possible to re-write the quadratic in the form:

\begin{equation*} y=a(x-x_1)^2 \end{equation*}

Logarithmic Functions

The Logarithm Function

The logarithm function is written as follows: Logarithm function

\begin{equation*} y=\log _a (x), \end{equation*}

where \(a\) and \(x\) are positive and \(a \neq 1\) is constant.

This can be interpreted as:

“\(y\) is the power to which one must raise \(a\) (the base), to get \(x\) (the argument).”

That is:

\begin{equation*} a^y = x \end{equation*}

Introduction

For example, the two statements

\begin{equation*} 16=2^4 \quad \text{and} \quad \log _2 (16) = 4 \end{equation*}

are equivalent.

Logarithms are used to simplify calculations and determine unknown powers (exponents).

Calculate \(x\):

  • \(3^x = 81\)

  • \(6^x = 1\)

  • \(2^x = 0.125\)

Examples

Calculate \(x\):

  • \(3^x = 81\) \(\therefore \ \ x=\log _3 (81) =4\)

  • \(6^x = 1\) \(\therefore \ \ x=\log _6 (1) =0\)

  • \(2^x = 0.125\) \(\therefore \ \ x=\log _2 (0.125) =-3\)

So one application of logs is to solve equations where the desired variable is in the index.

Logarithms

The most commonly used bases are 10 and \(\operatorname {e}\).

The log button on your calculator is \(\log _{10}\). This is the common logarithm.

The \(\ln \) button on your calculator is \(\log _{\operatorname {e}}\). This is the natural logarithm and is usually written \(\ln \), so:

\begin{equation*} \log _{\operatorname {e}} (x) = \ln (x) \end{equation*}

Engineers mainly deal with the natural logarithm.

Exponential Functions

Introduction - Exponential Functions

General exponential functions (with base \(b\)) are of the form:

\begin{equation*} y= A b^{kx} \end{equation*}

where \(A,b\) and \(k\) are constants:

  • \(A\) is a coefficient.

  • \(b\) is the base.

  • \(k\) determines how fast the function grows (growth rate).

Certain values for the base \(b\) are more common than others, especially

\begin{equation*} y=10^x \ \ \ \text{and} \ \ \ y=\operatorname {e}^x=\text{exp}(x) \end{equation*}

THE Exponential Function

One particular base is very important: \(\operatorname {e} = 2.718281828\), called Euler’s number.

The general form of this type of equation is: Exponential function:

\begin{equation*} y=A\operatorname {e}^{Bx} +C \end{equation*}

where \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are constants.

Note, when \(x=0\), \(y=A+C\) (this is the \(y\)-intercept).

Visualising Exponential Functions

Changing the sign of \(B\) reflects the \(\operatorname {e}^x\) curve in the \(y\)-axis: positive \(B\) gives exponential growth, negative \(B\) gives decay.

Applications of the Exponential Function

The exponential function is used frequently across engineering.

It is used in growth and decay models such as:

  • Tension in belts: \(T_1=T_0 \operatorname {e}^{\mu \theta }\)

  • Newton’s law of cooling: \(\theta = \theta _0 \operatorname {e}^{-kt}\)

  • Atmospheric pressure at altitude \(h\): \(p=p_0 \operatorname {e}^{-h/c}\)

  • Discharge of a capacitor: \(q=Q\operatorname {e}^{-t/CR}\)

Logarithms and Exponentials

The logarithm and exponential functions are the inverses of each other, i.e. one undoes the impact of the other:

\(\ln (\operatorname {e}^x)=x\) and \(\operatorname {e}^{\ln (x)}=x\)

Example:

\(\ln (\operatorname {e}^7)=7\) and \(\operatorname {e}^{\ln (15)}=15\)

Example 2 - Solution

Substitute the values:

\begin{equation*} 6 = 12 \operatorname {e}^{\left(-\frac{t}{200 \times 10^{3} \times 500 \times 10^{-9}}\right)} \end{equation*}

Simplifying:

\begin{equation*} \frac{6}{12} = \operatorname {e}^{\left(\frac{-t}{10^{-1}}\right)} \hspace{5mm} \implies \hspace{5mm} \frac{1}{2} = \operatorname {e}^{-10t} \end{equation*}

\begin{equation*} \ln \bigg( \frac{1}{2} \bigg) = \ln \big( \operatorname {e}^{-10t} \big) \hspace{5mm} \implies \hspace{5mm} t = -\frac{1}{10} \ln \bigg( \frac{1}{2} \bigg) = 0.069\dots \end{equation*}
